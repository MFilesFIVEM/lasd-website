Department Manual & Directives

Standard Operating Procedures (SOP)

Comprehensive tactical protocols, use of force regulations, radio communication guidelines, legal case law precedents, and specialized bureau procedures for all LASD personnel. Click any category card to expand full details.

1. Patrol Operations

  • Shift Protocol: Sign on duty via radio as 10-8 with assigned designator and callsign before leaving station.
  • Traffic Stops (10-38): Provide location, license plate, and occupant count to dispatch prior to approach.
  • Patrol Units: Single-person units may patrol standard sectors; two-person units recommended for high-risk beats.
Click to view full Patrol SOP

2. Pursuit Directives (10-80)

  • Initiation: Broadcast direction, speed, vehicle description, and reason for pursuit immediately.
  • Supervisor Control: Primary supervisor must assume tactical control of the pursuit channel and authorize PIT maneuvers.
  • Air Support: Air1 assumes primary overhead tracking once on station; ground units drop back to secondary spacing.
Click to view full Pursuit Directives

3. Use of Force Continuum

  • Proportionality: Force used must be objectively reasonable based on the totality of circumstances.
  • Less-Lethal: TASER and beanbag shotguns authorized for active physical resistance or fleeing suspects presenting safety risks.
  • Reporting: Any discharge of firearm or use of intermediate force requires a detailed Internal Use of Force report within 24 hours.
Click to view Use of Force Policy

4. Specialized Units (SEB / OSS)

  • Hostage / Barricade: SEB captain or senior tactical lead assumes command of inner perimeter upon arrival.
  • Warrant Service: High-risk search warrants must be cleared through OSS and tactical commanders 1 hour prior.
  • K-9 Deployment: Canine units must issue clear verbal warnings before deploying for suspect apprehension.
Click to view Specialized Bureau SOP

5. Radio Etiquette & Codes

  • Channel Discipline: Keep radio traffic concise. State callsign, wait for acknowledgment before proceeding.
  • Emergency Traffic: 10-33 clears channel for priority operations. 10-99 indicates officer down/stolen.
  • Full 10-Code Index: Complete reference table available in expanded SOP view.
Click to view Radio Codes & Guide

6. Standards & Discipline

  • Strike System: Department operates on a 3-strike disciplinary policy administered by Internal Affairs or High Command.
  • Leave of Absence (LOA): Deputies must notify supervisory staff via Discord/Roster update prior to extended inactivity.
  • Professionalism: Officers are expected to maintain courteous conduct with civilians and uphold department reputation at all times.
Click to view Disciplinary Standards

7. Case Law Precedents

  • Person Guidelines: Terry v. Ohio, Illinois v. Wardlow, Tennessee v. Garner, & more.
  • Vehicle Operations: Arizona v. Gant, Pennsylvania v. Mimms, Whren v. US.
  • Residences & Warrants: Florida v. Jardines, Chimel v. California.

7. Law Enforcement Case Laws

Official Judicial Precedents & Constitutional Guidelines

Person

Colten v. Kentucky, 407 U.S. 104 (1972) Link

Individuals do not have a constitutional right to observe a traffic stop nor engage with the Law Enforcement Officer conducting the traffic, and may be arrested for interfering.

Example: While on a traffic stop, an individual, not involved in the traffic stop, failed to follow a Law Enforcement Officer's lawful orders to leave and was thus arrested for disorderly conduct.
Draper v. United States, 358 U.S. 307 (1959) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may conduct a Warrantless Arrest of an individual stated to have committed a crime or committing a crime based on information from an informant.

Example: An informant informs you about an individual transporting narcotics and provides a description, you locate this individual and arrest them, finding the narcotics.
Illinois v. Lafayette, 462 U.S. 640 (1983) Link

Law Enforcement Officers can search the personal effects of an individual under a lawful arrest.

Example: An individual is arrested for Disturbing The Peace. A search of the person's bag then locates an illegal firearm.
Illinois v. Wardlow, 528 U.S. 119 (2000) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may conduct a brief, investigatory stop as long as the officer has reasonable suspicion that crime may be afoot.

Example: An individual in a high crime area flees upon seeing Law Enforcement and is subsequently detained for further investigation.
Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347 (1967) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer needs Advanced Authorization, i.e. a Warrant to conduct electronic surveillance, i.e. bugging someone's home or phone. This includes public phones.

Maryland v. King, 569 U.S. 435 (2013) Link

Following a Lawful Arrest supported by Probable Cause, Law Enforcement can take a cheek swab of the suspect's DNA, similar to fingerprinting and photographing.

Michigan v. Chesternut, 486 U.S. 567 (1988) Link

If a Law Enforcement Officer "gives chase," without inhibiting the individual's freedom of movement (i.e. Ordering them to stop, activating sirens), of an individual to further investigate their actions and discover criminal activity the Law Enforcement Officer can arrest the individual.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer observes an individual flee upon the sight of Law Enforcement. While investigating, the Law Enforcement Officer observes the individual discard packets that are later confirmed to contain a controlled substance and subsequently arrests and searches the individual.
Minnesota v. Dickerson, 508 U.S. 366 (1993) Link

Extension of Terry v. Ohio, which allows a Law Enforcement Officer to seize Nonthreatening contraband (i.e. Narcotics) detected through touch during a non-invasive pat down.

Rawlings v. Kentucky, 448 U.S. 98 (1980) Link

If an individual claims ownership of items within the personal effects of another individual, it gives Law Enforcement Officers Probable Cause to search the owner and their personal effects. Subsequently, the owner of the illicit items cannot deny consent for Law Enforcement to search the personal effects of another individual.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer is given consent to search a backpack and locate narcotics. An individual claims ownership of those narcotics that were discovered in the backpack, but is not the owner of the backpack and therefore cannot contest consent to search the backpack.
Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer cannot search the digital information on an arrested individual's cell phone without a warrant.

Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973) Link

Law Enforcement must demonstrate that consent to search was given voluntarily.

Tennessee v. Garner, 471 U.S. 1 (1985) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may shoot and kill a fleeing individual if that Officer believes that their escape poses great risk of serious bodily injury or death to the public or the Law Enforcement Officer.

Example: A known Serial Killer is unarmed and fleeing from a Law Enforcement Officer, fearing for the public's safety should the person escape, the Law Enforcement Officer shoots and kills the fleeing suspect.
Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may stop and "frisk," which is a non-invasive search by patting down the outer clothing, and then search further upon locating what may be a weapon; this applies to narcotics as well. This can be conducted on individuals that may be in the process of committing a crime.

Example: You observe a person that is passing a storefront numerous times, seemingly casing the business. Upon confrontation, you conduct a frisk search for Officer Safety and feel a weapon, which gives you justification to further investigate what may be a weapon.
United States v. Robinson, 414 U.S. 218 (1973) Link

A search following the Lawful Arrest of an individual is an exception to the warrant requirement under the Fourth Amendment and is also considered a Reasonable Search.

United States v. Sharpe, 470 U.S. 675 (1985) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer, with Reasonable Suspicion, may detain an individual to conduct an investigation. The Law Enforcement Officer can hold an individual in detainment for an extended period of time, as long as they are diligently pursuing their investigation and causing any unnecessary delay.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer pulls a vehicle over for another Law Enforcement Officer conducting an investigation and detains the individual for when the investigating Law Enforcement Officer arrives to continue their investigation.

Vehicle

Arizona v. Gant, 556 U.S. 332 (2009) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may search the passenger compartment of an arrested individual if it is reasonable that the individual may access the vehicle during the search, or if the vehicle contains evidence of the offense of the arrest.

Arizona v. Johnson, 555 U.S. 323 (2009) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may order a passenger out of a vehicle and then may conduct a pat down of the individual for weapons should the Law Enforcement Officer believe that they possess weapons, under the pretense of Officer Safety.

Byrd v. United States, 584 U.S. 395 (2018) Link

An individual that has lawful possession and control of a vehicle, despite not being the owner of the vehicle, has the expectation of privacy to the same degree as the owner.

Example: An individual, not listed on an agreement of authorized users, operating a rental car with the permission of the renter, has the same expectation of privacy.
Brendlin v. California, 551 U.S. 249 (2007) Link

All individuals in a vehicle are lawfully detained by a Law Enforcement Officer during a traffic stop and may not leave the traffic stop until being told so by the Law Enforcement Officer.

Cady v. Dombrowski, 413 U.S. 433 (1973) Link

A Warrantless Search of a vehicle can be conducted should Law Enforcement exercise some form of custody over a vehicle.

Example: A vehicle is taken into police custody following a drunk driving accident and is searched, locating illicit items.
California v. Acevedo, 500 U.S. 565 (1991) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may search a container inside a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe that it contains illicit items or evidence.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer observes an individual leave a building known for narcotics trafficking with a bag and place that bag in the car. Upon stopping the car, the Law Enforcement Officer can search the bag.
California v. Carney, 471 U.S. 386 (1985) Link

A Motor Home (i.e. RV) falls within the vehicle exception to the Fourth Amendment as a Motor Home is readily mobile and has a reduced expectation of privacy due to pervasive vehicle regulations.

Chambers v. Maroney, 399 U.S. 42 (1970) Link

If a Law Officer had probable cause to arrest individuals within a vehicle, they have probable cause to search the vehicle without a warrant.

Example: You stop a vehicle based on the description of an individual that was a victim of a crime. Inside, the Law Enforcement Officer discovers the suspects inside the vehicle and subsequently arrests the individuals and can search the vehicle in its entirety.
Collins v. Virginia, 584 U.S. 586 (2018) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer cannot enter a home or the curtilage (i.e. a partially enclosed portion connected to the building) to search a vehicle under the exception to vehicles under the Fourth Amendment.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer cannot walk up the driveway of a home and remove the tarp covering a vehicle to identify the vehicle as stolen.
Delaware v. Prouse, 440 U.S. 648 (1979) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may not stop a vehicle simply to see if the driver has their license if the vehicle has not committed any traffic violations, equipment violations, or any suspicious activity.

Illinois v. Caballes, 543 U.S. 405 (2005) Link

A K9 Officer conducting a dog sniff of a lawfully stopped vehicle, which reveals an illegal substance, does not violate the Fourth Amendment.

Kansas v. Glover, 589 U.S. 376 (2020) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may conduct an investigative traffic stop after learning that the registered owner of the vehicle has had their license suspended.

Maryland v. Dyson, 527 U.S. 465 (1999) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer can conduct a Warrantless Search of a vehicle if the probable cause for searching that vehicle would justify issuing a warrant.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer receives a tip that a vehicle is transporting narcotics, subsequently they stop and search the vehicle under the Probable Cause that narcotics are present in the vehicle.
Maryland v. Pringle, 540 U.S. 366 (2003) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer, after discovering illegal contraband inside a vehicle, may arrest one or all individuals inside of a vehicle. Due to the nature of a vehicle an individual inside a vehicle is more than likely to be aware of narcotics within the passenger compartment.

Example: After searching a vehicle a Law Enforcement Officer locates cocaine inside the passenger compartment of a vehicle, with no one taking ownership of the narcotics the officer arrests and charges one or more of the occupants for the narcotics.
Maryland v. Wilson, 519 U.S. 408 (1997) Link

As an extension of Pennsylvania v. Mimms, a Law Enforcement Officer may order passengers to exit a vehicle and states that passengers have "no Fourth Amendment Right to not be ordered out of a vehicle."

Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer, while conducting a protective search of a vehicle for weapons, cannot be required to ignore any illegal contraband that he locates as the search was initiated on grounds of probable danger to the officer.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer stops an intoxicated person while driving and sees a knife in plain view, while conducting a search for any additional weapons the Officer locates cocaine and subsequently arrests the suspect on those charges as well.
Ohio v. Robinette, 519 U.S. 33 (1996) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer does not need to advise a lawfully detained individual that he is "free to go" before the Officer obtains consent to search a vehicle.

Pennsylvania v. Labron, 518 U.S. 938 (1996) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer can conduct a Warrantless Search of a vehicle if the Officer has sufficient probable cause.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer observes individuals engaging in narcotics transactions from a vehicle and subsequently stops the vehicle and searches the vehicle locating narcotics inside the vehicle.
Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U.S. 106 (1977) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may order an individual out of a vehicle upon the ground of Officer Safety.

Robbins v. California, 453 U.S. 420 (1981) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may search a container in a vehicle, whose outward appearance infers what the contents of the package are (i.e. a package that is similarly packaged to narcotics.)

Example: During a traffic stop a Law Enforcement Officer locates a package that is packaged similarly to cocaine can subsequently search that package based on the package's outward appearance.
Rodriguez v. United States, 575 U.S. 348 (2015) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer conducting a traffic stop, after completing all everything relating to a stop (i.e. checking the license, issuing a citation.), cannot extend a traffic stop in order to conduct a dog sniff.

South Dakota v. Opperman, 428 U.S. 364 (1976) Link

When a vehicle is lawfully impounded a Law Enforcement Officer may conduct an inventory search of the vehicle, any subsequent contraband may be then lawfully used as evidence in an arrest of the vehicle owner/operator.

United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. 798 (1982) Link

Law Enforcement Officers that have lawfully stopped a vehicle and have probable cause that contraband may be concealed in the vehicle may conduct a Warrantless Search of the vehicle.

Virginia v. Moore, 553 U.S. 164 (2008) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer can search an individual's vehicle following a lawful arrest.

Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806 (1996) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer, detaining a motorist under the probable cause of a traffic violation and subsequent findings of contraband does not violate the Fourth Amendment.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer observes a vehicle which they believe to be speeding and prompt initiates a traffic stop and then observes contraband in the vehicle, allowing them to conduct a full search of the vehicle.
Wyoming v. Houghton, 526 U.S. 295 (1999) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer with probable cause for a vehicle search may inspect the personal belongings of an individual as those belongings are capable of concealing the focus of the search.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer discovers a magazine for a firearm in the center console of a vehicle and can then search the personal belongings in an effort to find the firearm.

Home / Place of Residence

Bailey v. United States, 568 U.S. 186 (2013) Link

Upheld the ruling of Michigan v. Summers, that a Law Enforcement Officer, conducting a search warrant of a building, may only detain individuals within the immediate vicinity of the building.

California v. Ciraolo, 476 U.S. 207 (1986) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer can obtain a search warrant for a house based on the naked-eye aerial observation by a Law Enforcement Officer.

Example: A Law Enforcement Officer flying over a property observes a drug manufacturing operation and can then secure a Warrant to search the property based on their observation.
California v. Greenwood, 486 U.S. 35 (1988) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer may conduct a Warrantless Search of garbage that has been left outside a building, beyond the curtilage of a building, for collection.

Chimel v. California, 395 U.S. 752 (1969) Link

Even if a Law Enforcement Officer possesses a valid Arrest Warrant they cannot conduct a search of a building under the pretense of "search incident to arrest."

Fernandez v. California, 571 U.S. 292 (2014) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer gains consent to search a premises from one occupant, who holds common authority over the premises, the Law Enforcement Officer cannot conduct a Warrantless Search if another present party denies consent to search.

Example: Law Enforcement Officers obtain consent to search a building from one individual that lives at a house, but another denies consent. Thus, Law Enforcement Officers cannot search the house.
Florida v. Jardines, 569 U.S. 1 (2013) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer cannot bring a K9 onto an individual's premises as doing so constitutes a Warrantless Search.

Georgia v. Randolph, 547 U.S. 103 (2006) Link

A Law Enforcement Officer cannot conduct a warrantless search of a premises if one occupant of the premises denies consent to search the premises.

Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983) Link

Established the "totality-of-the-circumstances" test for determining whether an informant's tip provides probable cause for a search warrant.
